# The DISCOVER Practitioner Mindset

> **Purpose:** Make the reasoning of an experienced discovery practitioner explicit so it can be learned, challenged and reused.

---

# 1. Discovery Is the Disciplined Pursuit of Understanding

Discovery is not interviewing.

Discovery is not requirements gathering.

Discovery is not documentation.

Discovery is the disciplined pursuit of understanding so that better decisions can be made with sufficient confidence.

The practitioner is not trying to collect answers.

The practitioner is trying to reduce important uncertainty.

---

# 2. Think in Uncertainty, Not Questions

Before asking:

> "What question should I ask?"

ask:

> **"What do I not yet understand?"**

Questions are tools for reducing uncertainty.

Example:

Weak approach:

> "What systems do you use?"

Practitioner thinking:

> "I do not yet understand where the information used in this decision originates or where technology constrains the process."

Now a system question has a purpose.

---

# 3. Ask the Question Behind the Question

Stakeholder:

> "We need a dashboard."

Practitioner asks internally:

- What decision is currently difficult?
- What information is missing?
- Is the problem availability, trust, definition or timing?
- What action would change?
- What value would the dashboard create?

The proposed solution becomes evidence about a need, not the requirement itself.

---

# 4. Separate What Is Said From What Is Known

Stakeholder:

> "The data quality is terrible."

Possible classifications:

- Stakeholder Statement: data quality is perceived as poor.
- Evidence: 18% of records fail a validated completeness rule.
- Interpretation: missing records may contribute to reconciliation.
- Assumption: the data-quality issue is the root cause.

Do not collapse these into:

> "Root cause = bad data."

---

# 5. Follow the Decision

When discovery becomes vague, find the decision.

Ask:

- What decision is being made?
- Who makes it?
- What triggers it?
- What information is required?
- What rule applies?
- What action follows?
- What outcome results?

This often reveals what data actually matters.

---

# 6. Follow the Value

When discovery becomes busy, ask:

> **"So what?"**

Examples:

- Why does this report exist?
- Why does this process need to be faster?
- Why does this definition matter?
- Why would this data product be useful?
- Why should AI be introduced?

Keep tracing until a meaningful outcome, risk or value is reached.

---

# 7. Look for Contradiction

Contradiction is useful.

Examples:

- Finance says Sales owns the number.
- Sales says Finance owns it.
- Two teams define "revenue" differently.
- The documented process differs from actual practice.
- A system is described as authoritative but users routinely override it.

Do not smooth the contradiction away.

Mark it.

Ask what evidence can resolve it.

---

# 8. Look for Exceptions

The happy path shows how the process is intended to work.

Exceptions often show how the business really works.

Ask:

- What happens when this is late?
- What happens when the contract is unusual?
- What happens when the data is missing?
- What happens when someone disagrees?
- Who can override?
- Which cases require spreadsheets?

Exceptions reveal:

- hidden rules;
- specialist knowledge;
- process weakness;
- risk;
- semantic ambiguity.

---

# 9. Distinguish Symptom From Root Cause

Symptom:

> "The report takes too long."

Possible causes:

- upstream approval delay;
- conflicting definitions;
- manual reconciliation;
- late source data;
- insufficient capacity;
- system limitation.

Do not stop at the symptom.

But also do not claim a root cause without evidence.

---

# 10. Prefer the Smallest Useful Next Step

Discovery can expand indefinitely.

An experienced practitioner asks:

> **"What is the smallest next activity that would materially increase confidence?"**

Examples:

- interview one process owner;
- validate one disputed definition;
- observe one reconciliation;
- profile one dataset;
- review one business rule;
- run one focused workshop.

Do not schedule four workshops if one targeted evidence check will answer the question.

---

# 11. Know When to Go Deeper

Go deeper when:

- the uncertainty is high-impact;
- stakeholders disagree;
- a rule affects money/risk/compliance;
- a data product depends on the answer;
- an AI system would act on the information;
- the finding materially changes the roadmap.

Stay high-level when:

- the detail does not affect the current decision;
- sufficient evidence already exists;
- the area is out of scope.

---

# 12. Know When to Stop

Stop when:

- the current decision can be made with sufficient confidence;
- critical assumptions have been tested;
- material contradictions are resolved or explicitly carried;
- remaining unknowns are understood and non-blocking;
- further discovery has diminishing value.

Do not stop because:

- the workshop ended;
- a template is complete;
- the project plan says discovery is finished.

---

# 13. Triangulate Important Findings

For high-impact findings, seek more than one form of evidence where practical.

Examples:

- stakeholder interview;
- policy/document;
- observed process;
- operational data;
- system behaviour;
- second stakeholder perspective.

Triangulation increases confidence and exposes contradictions.

---

# 14. Ask for Examples

When someone says:

- always;
- never;
- everyone;
- normally;
- the system;
- bad data;
- too slow;
- too manual;

ask:

> "Can you give me a recent example?"

Examples turn abstract complaints into discoverable evidence.

---

# 15. Model the Business, Then Map the Systems

First understand:

- capability;
- process;
- decision;
- information;
- rule.

Then ask:

- which system represents it?
- where is the data?
- how does it move?
- where is the technical constraint?

This keeps business meaning durable.

---

# 16. Treat Workarounds as Evidence

A spreadsheet, manual adjustment or unofficial process is not automatically bad.

Ask:

- What need does it satisfy?
- Why does the official process not satisfy that need?
- What rule or knowledge is encoded in it?
- What would break if it disappeared?

Workarounds often reveal missing business capability.

---

# 17. Preserve Context

The same term can legitimately mean different things in different contexts.

Do not standardise prematurely.

Ask:

- Where is this definition valid?
- Who owns it?
- How does another context interpret it?
- Is translation required?

This is particularly important for Finance, Rights, Sales and Marketing.

---

# 18. Challenge Precision

A number can create false authority.

If someone says:

> "This will save £2m."

ask:

- What is the baseline?
- How was the number calculated?
- Which assumptions does it depend on?
- Who owns the benefit?
- What evidence exists?

Use qualitative value if quantitative evidence is not available.

---

# 19. Challenge Technology-Led Framing

Request:

> "We need to replace System X."

Ask:

- Which business capability is constrained?
- Which process or decision is affected?
- What information problem exists?
- Which business outcome would improve?
- What evidence proves the constraint?

The system may still need replacement.

But the recommendation should be business-traceable.

---

# 20. Challenge AI-Led Framing

Request:

> "We need an AI assistant."

Ask:

- Which user task?
- Which decision?
- Which information?
- Which rules?
- What must remain human-controlled?
- What does success mean?
- What non-AI alternative exists?

AI is an intervention hypothesis.

---

# 21. Think in Golden Threads

For a material opportunity ask:

```text
Why?
STR / CAP / VAL
        ↓
Where?
PRC
        ↓
What decision?
DEC
        ↓
What information / rules?
INF / BR
        ↓
What enables it?
SYS / DAT / DP
        ↓
What action changes?
ACT
        ↓
What value results?
VAL
```

If a link is missing, that is a useful discovery question.

---

# 22. Find Broken Threads

Examples:

- report exists but no decision is known;
- KPI exists but no owner;
- process exists but no value measure;
- data product proposed but no consumer;
- AI idea exists but no user task;
- business rule exists only in code;
- strategic objective has no capability ownership.

Broken Threads tell the practitioner where to investigate next.

---

# 23. Make Reasoning Visible

When recommending a workshop or technique, explain:

> "I recommend Capability Mapping because the business domain is unfamiliar and we do not yet have a stable view of what Finance must be able to do."

This teaches the practitioner.

It also makes AI recommendations challengeable.

---

# 24. Prioritise High-Leverage Questions

A high-leverage question unlocks several areas of understanding.

Example:

> "What are the most important decisions Finance makes every month?"

Potentially reveals:

- people;
- process;
- information;
- rules;
- KPIs;
- technology;
- value.

Prefer these before low-level inventory questions.

---

# 25. Move From Broad to Deep

A useful pattern:

```text
Outcome
   ↓
Capability
   ↓
Process
   ↓
Decision
   ↓
Information / Rule
   ↓
Technology / Data
```

Do not begin by cataloguing fields if you do not understand why they matter.

---

# 26. Remain Comfortable With UNKNOWN

An explicit unknown is a successful discovery outcome when it identifies what must be learned next.

Use:

> `UNKNOWN — requires validation with Financial Control`

This is stronger than a plausible invented answer.

---

# 27. Practise Semantic Listening

Ontology thinking should be present throughout discovery even when ontology creation is not the objective.

A DISCOVER practitioner listens not only for what stakeholders say about their work, but also for clues about the underlying business meaning.

This practice is called **Semantic Listening**.

> **Semantic Listening is the practice of identifying business concepts, relationships, rules, states, events and terminology while conducting normal discovery activities. It allows ontology knowledge to emerge progressively without turning every discovery activity into an ontology workshop.**

Semantic Listening is an **always-on background lens**.

POPIT-V remains the visible structure of the discovery.

Semantic Listening operates underneath it.

```text
Visible Discovery
────────────────────────────
People
Organisation
Process
Information
Technology
Value

        ↓ listen beneath ↓

Semantic Clues
────────────────────────────
Concepts
Relationships
Definitions
Rules
Events
States
Classifications
Identifiers
Ownership
Terminology
Constraints
```

## What to Listen for Across POPIT-V

### People

Listen for:

- roles;
- actors;
- ownership;
- authority;
- responsibility;
- specialist knowledge.

Possible semantic questions:

- Who owns this concept?
- Who is authorised to change it?
- Who performs this role?
- Is the role different from the person or team currently performing it?

### Organisation

Listen for:

- domains;
- capabilities;
- organisational entities;
- ownership boundaries;
- hierarchies;
- governance relationships.

Possible semantic questions:

- Is this a business capability, team or process?
- Does the meaning change between business areas?
- Which domain owns the concept?

### Process

Listen for:

- events;
- state changes;
- decisions;
- relationships;
- rules;
- exceptions.

Possible semantic questions:

- What happens to this object when the event occurs?
- What states can it move through?
- What rule controls the transition?
- Can one instance relate to many others?

### Information

Listen for:

- concepts;
- definitions;
- attributes;
- classifications;
- identifiers;
- synonyms;
- KPIs;
- business rules.

Possible semantic questions:

- What does this term mean here?
- Is that the same concept as the term another team uses?
- What uniquely identifies it?
- Are there different types?
- Which attributes describe it?

### Technology

Listen for:

- system-specific names;
- identifiers;
- physical representations;
- source mappings;
- duplication;
- technical constraints.

Possible semantic questions:

- Is the system name the same as the business concept?
- Does another system represent the same concept differently?
- Which identifier can be used across systems?
- Is a technical field carrying a business rule?

### Value

Listen for:

- outcomes;
- measures;
- KPIs;
- relationships between actions and outcomes;
- definitions of success.

Possible semantic questions:

- What exactly is being measured?
- Which business concept does the measure describe?
- What action is expected to influence the outcome?

---

## Ontology Trigger Questions

Use these naturally when the conversation provides a clue.

When an important noun appears:

> **"What does that mean in your context?"**

When two terms appear similar:

> **"Are those the same thing, or are they genuinely different concepts?"**

When a relationship appears:

> **"Can one of these relate to several of those, or is it always one-to-one?"**

When categories appear:

> **"What different types are there?"**

When a lifecycle appears:

> **"What states can this move through?"**

When a rule appears:

> **"Is that always true, or are there exceptions?"**

When meaning is disputed:

> **"Who owns or approves that definition?"**

When a system term appears:

> **"Is that the business term, or just what the system calls it?"**

The facilitator does not need to use ontology terminology with participants.

The purpose is to uncover meaning without distracting from the business conversation.

---

## Capture Candidates, Not Certainties

Early semantic notes should be treated as candidates.

For example:

```text
[C] Distribution Contract
[C] Deal
[C] Revenue
[C] Delivery Schedule

[R] Distribution Contract governs Deal
[R] Deal generates Revenue
[R] Revenue follows Delivery Schedule

[EVT] Contract Signed

[RULE] Revenue recognition depends on delivery schedule

[OQ] Can one contract govern multiple deals?
[OWN] Revenue Recognition definition — UNKNOWN
```

These observations should not automatically become ontology truth.

They require:

- evidence;
- validation;
- context;
- ownership.

If unsupported, retain them as candidates or open questions.

---

## Progressive Semantic Formalisation

Move from lightweight discovery to formal modelling only as confidence and need increase.

```text
Semantic Clues
      ↓
Candidate Concepts / Relationships
      ↓
Business Glossary
      ↓
Concept Model
      ↓
Validated Business Ontology
      ↓
Source / Data Mapping
      ↓
Formal Semantic Implementation where justified
```

Do not begin with RDF, OWL, graph technology or source schemas unless the discovery objective genuinely requires them.

Business meaning comes first.

---

## Relationship to the Golden Thread

The Golden Thread and ontology answer different but complementary questions.

The Golden Thread provides **business traceability**:

```text
Capability
   ↓
Process
   ↓
Decision
   ↓
Information
   ↓
Rule
   ↓
Technology / Data
   ↓
Action
   ↓
Value
```

Ontology provides **business meaning and relationships** within and across those elements.

Example:

```text
CAP-001 Financial Planning
        ↓
PRC-004 Forecasting
        ↓
DEC-007 Approve Forecast
        ↓
INF-012 Forecast
        ↓
BR-006 Forecast Approval Rule
```

Semantic Listening then asks:

- What exactly is a Forecast?
- How does a Forecast relate to a Production?
- Can a Production have several Forecasts?
- What is a Forecast Version?
- What makes a Forecast "Approved"?
- Which Territory or Label owns it?
- Who owns the definition?

The Golden Thread tells us **why the information matters**.

The ontology tells us **what the information means and how it relates to the rest of the domain**.

---

## Semantic Listening Self-Check

During or immediately after a discovery session ask:

- Which important concepts appeared?
- Which relationships did stakeholders describe?
- Which terms were ambiguous?
- Which terms had multiple names?
- Which rules were implied?
- Which events changed business state?
- Which classifications or hierarchies appeared?
- Which identifiers were mentioned?
- Who appears to own the meaning?
- Which semantic questions remain unanswered?

Do not attempt to answer unsupported questions.

Capture them as `OQ-*` or `UNKNOWN` and use them to guide later discovery.

---

# 28. Practitioner Self-Check

At any moment ask:

1. What am I trying to understand?
2. Why does it matter?
3. What evidence do I currently have?
4. What am I assuming?
5. What conflicts?
6. What is the next most valuable uncertainty?
7. Which technique is the cheapest reliable way to reduce it?
8. Which artefact should change?
9. Who can validate?
10. When will I know enough?

---

# 29. Guidance for AI Assistants

When generating discovery advice:

Do:

- expose reasoning;
- explain why lenses/techniques are selected;
- prioritise questions;
- identify evidence expected;
- show artefacts to update;
- flag assumptions;
- surface Broken Threads;
- define exit criteria.

Do not:

- dump every possible question;
- recommend every POPIT-V lens equally;
- invent domain facts;
- assume technology;
- force complete Golden Threads;
- confuse document completion with confidence.

---

# 30. The Practitioner Mindset in One Sentence

> **Do not ask what DISCOVER tells you to do next. Ask what you need to understand next, then use DISCOVER to learn it.**
