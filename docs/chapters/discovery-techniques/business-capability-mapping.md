# Business Capability Mapping

> *"Start with what the organisation must be able to do, not how it is currently organised or which systems it happens to use."*

## Purpose

Business Capability Mapping describes **what an organisation must be able to do to achieve its outcomes**, independently from the current organisation chart, processes or technology.

Within DISCOVER, capability mapping provides a durable bridge between strategy and operational discovery. It helps practitioners organise a complex business domain before diving into process, information or systems.

## Why This Matters

Without a capability view, discovery can become organised around:

- departments;
- applications;
- individual projects;
- reports;
- existing process documents.

Those structures change frequently and can hide duplicated activity or unclear ownership.

A capability model creates a more stable lens for questions such as:

- What must the business be good at?
- Which capabilities are strategically differentiating?
- Which capabilities are weak or fragmented?
- Which capabilities depend on poor information?
- Where should data products or AI capabilities be aligned?
- Where is ownership unclear?

## What a Capability Is

A business capability describes an **ability the organisation needs**.

Examples:

- Manage Content Rights
- Sell Content Licences
- Recognise Revenue
- Forecast Financial Performance
- Manage Customer Relationships
- Measure Content Performance

A capability is not:

- a team;
- a process;
- a system;
- a project;
- a report;
- an individual task.

## Capability vs Process vs Organisation vs Technology

| Concept | Question | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Capability | What must we be able to do? | Forecast Financial Performance |
| Process | How is the outcome achieved? | Monthly Forecasting Process |
| Organisation | Who performs / owns it? | FP&A |
| Technology | What enables it? | Planning Platform |
| Information | What meaning is required? | Forecast, Actual, Variance |

This distinction is foundational to DISCOVER's principle **Model the Business, Not the Systems**.

## How Capability Mapping Fits DISCOVER

Capability mapping is most useful during:

- **Define** — frame the business area and outcome;
- **Investigate** — establish the landscape;
- **Study** — select priority capabilities for deeper discovery;
- **Organise** — structure findings and ownership;
- **Evaluate** — assess maturity and opportunity;
- **Roadmap** — organise change around capability outcomes.

It should rarely be treated as a one-off diagram.

## POPIT-V Connections

A capability can be examined through all POPIT-V perspectives:

- **People** — who owns and performs it?
- **Organisation** — where does it sit and how is it governed?
- **Process** — which processes realise it?
- **Information** — what information and decisions are essential?
- **Technology** — what enables or constrains it?
- **Value** — what outcome does the capability create?

## When to Use It

Use capability mapping when:

- the business area is complex;
- several teams contribute to the same outcome;
- the organisation chart is misleading;
- systems have become proxies for business domains;
- you need a stable structure for a roadmap;
- you need to identify ownership or maturity;
- you are building a domain/data product portfolio;
- you need to connect strategy to operational change.

## When Not to Use It

Do not build an elaborate capability hierarchy when:

- the discovery scope is already very narrow;
- a simple context diagram answers the question;
- the immediate problem is a specific process exception;
- stakeholders cannot yet agree the outcome or domain boundary.

The artefact should earn its keep.

## Naming Capabilities

Good capability names are usually:

- durable;
- business-oriented;
- outcome-neutral;
- independent from teams and systems;
- understandable to executives and practitioners.

Prefer:

- Manage Rights
- Plan Financial Performance
- Manage Royalties

Avoid:

- Rightsline Administration
- Finance Team
- Run Monthly Forecast Spreadsheet
- Build Dashboard

## Levels of Capability

A practical hierarchy may contain:

### Level 1 — Enterprise / Value-Chain Capabilities

Broad business abilities such as:

- Create Content
- Distribute Content
- Monetise Content
- Manage Enterprise Resources

### Level 2 — Domain Capabilities

Examples:

- Manage Content Rights
- Sell and License Content
- Manage Royalties
- Forecast Financial Performance

### Level 3 — Sub-capabilities

Examples under *Manage Content Rights*:

- Capture Rights
- Evaluate Availability
- Manage Restrictions
- Manage Windows
- Resolve Rights Conflicts

Avoid decomposition for its own sake. Stop when the level is sufficient for the discovery decision.

## Workshop Approach

### 1. Establish the Outcome

Ask:

- What business outcome are we trying to understand?
- Who receives value?
- Which strategic priorities matter?

### 2. Identify Candidate Capabilities

Ask stakeholders:

- What must the organisation be able to do to achieve that outcome?
- What abilities would still be needed if the organisation restructured tomorrow?
- Which abilities are business-critical?

Capture candidate capabilities quickly before debating the hierarchy.

### 3. Normalize the Language

Remove:

- team names;
- project names;
- system names;
- procedural wording.

Check whether two apparent capabilities are actually the same ability described differently.

### 4. Group and Decompose

Arrange capabilities into logical groups and levels.

Ask:

- Is this a distinct ability?
- Is it part of a wider ability?
- Does it have a different owner, purpose or maturity?
- Would splitting it improve a business decision?

### 5. Identify Ownership

Record:

- accountable owner;
- participating teams;
- governance;
- unresolved ownership.

### 6. Overlay Evidence

Only after the base model is understood, add overlays such as:

- maturity;
- pain points;
- strategic importance;
- data dependency;
- risk;
- opportunity;
- value;
- investment.

### 7. Validate

Validate the map with multiple stakeholder groups.

A capability model should create shared understanding rather than reflect the loudest participant.

## Discovery Questions

### Strategic Context

- Which capabilities directly enable the strategy?
- Which capabilities differentiate the organisation?
- Which capabilities are essential but non-differentiating?
- Which capabilities will become more important?

### Ownership

- Who owns this capability?
- What does ownership mean?
- Who owns performance?
- Which teams contribute?
- Where is accountability fragmented?

### Maturity

- Is the capability repeatable?
- Is information trusted?
- Is performance measured?
- Is the capability dependent on individuals?
- Is technology fit for purpose?
- Is value measured?

### Dependencies

- Which capabilities depend on this one?
- What information does it consume or produce?
- Which decisions cross capability boundaries?
- Which external partners are required?

### Pain Points

- Which capability generates the most complaints?
- Where is manual effort concentrated?
- Which capability is difficult to scale?
- Which capability creates downstream rework?

## Capability Heatmaps

A heatmap can overlay a specific dimension such as:

- strategic importance;
- maturity;
- business pain;
- risk;
- data quality;
- technology health;
- investment.

Do not combine several dimensions into one unexplained red/amber/green colour.

Always state:

- what is being assessed;
- evidence;
- scale;
- confidence;
- date.

## Finance Example

A Finance capability map might include:

**Financial Management**
- Financial Planning
- Forecast Financial Performance
- Manage Actuals
- Manage Revenue Recognition
- Manage Costs
- Manage Cash
- Manage Financial Reporting
- Manage Statutory / Regulatory Reporting
- Manage Tax

Discovery can then ask why *Forecast Financial Performance* is difficult without assuming the answer is the planning system.

A maturity overlay might reveal:

- process = Defined;
- information = Fragmented;
- technology = Managed;
- value = Developing.

That points discovery toward semantic and ownership problems rather than a platform replacement.

## Marketing Example

A Marketing capability map might include:

- Define Audience Strategy
- Plan Campaigns
- Manage Creative
- Manage Media Investment
- Activate Campaigns
- Measure Campaign Performance
- Optimise Marketing Activity
- Manage Customer Engagement

This structure can expose that several teams independently perform *Measure Campaign Performance* using different definitions.

## Connection to the Golden Thread

Capability mapping provides the first operational link after strategy:

`STR-001 → CAP-001`

From there, deeper discovery connects:

`CAP-001 → PRC-001 → DEC-001 → INF-001 → ... → VAL-001`

If the capability cannot be connected to an outcome or value, revisit why it is in scope.

## Common Pitfalls

### Capabilities as Departments

Bad:

- Finance
- Marketing
- Sales

These are organisation units, not necessarily capabilities.

### Capabilities as Processes

Bad:

- Run Monthly Close
- Approve Contract

These describe flow or activities.

### Capabilities as Systems

Bad:

- Salesforce
- Rightsline

Technology is an enabler, not the business ability.

### Over-Decomposition

A huge model with hundreds of tiny capabilities can create taxonomy work rather than insight.

### Designing the Future Too Early

Current capability understanding should precede debates about moving teams or replacing platforms.

## What Good Looks Like

A good capability model:

- uses business language;
- survives reorganisation;
- has clear scope;
- is at a useful level of detail;
- distinguishes ownership from participation;
- can accept maturity/value overlays;
- connects to the Golden Thread;
- supports prioritisation.

## Practitioner Checklist

- [ ] Outcome and scope are explicit.
- [ ] Capability names are business-oriented.
- [ ] Teams/processes/systems have not been used as capability names.
- [ ] Levels are proportionate.
- [ ] Owners are identified or marked unknown.
- [ ] Overlays have evidence and definitions.
- [ ] Strategic/value links are visible.
- [ ] Model has been validated by relevant stakeholders.

## Chapter Summary

Business Capability Mapping gives DISCOVER a stable view of what the organisation must be able to do. It creates structure for ownership, maturity, information, technology and value analysis without tying the business model to today's organisation chart or application landscape.
