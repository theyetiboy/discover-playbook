# Chapter 3 – Engagement Preparation

> *"Success in discovery is determined long before the first workshop begins."*

---

# 3.1 Introduction

Preparation is one of the most overlooked phases of discovery.

Many discovery engagements begin with workshops before sufficient context has been gathered. This often results in stakeholders spending valuable time explaining basic information rather than exploring genuine business problems and opportunities.

Effective preparation allows the discovery team to enter workshops informed, focused and ready to ask meaningful questions.

The objective is not to become an expert before speaking to the business.

The objective is to become informed enough to ask intelligent questions.

---

# 3.2 Objectives

The preparation phase aims to:

- Understand why the engagement exists
- Identify the business problem
- Define the scope
- Identify key stakeholders
- Review existing documentation
- Understand current initiatives
- Prepare workshop materials
- Develop an initial hypothesis

---

# 3.3 Inputs

Gather as much existing information as possible before engaging stakeholders.

## Business Documentation

- Business strategies
- Department objectives
- Operating model
- Organisation charts
- Annual plans
- Business cases

---

## Process Documentation

- Existing process maps
- Standard operating procedures
- User guides
- Training material

---

## Technology Documentation

- System landscape diagrams
- Application catalogues
- Integration diagrams
- Architecture documentation

---

## Data Documentation

- Data catalogues
- Business glossaries
- KPI definitions
- Existing reports
- Dashboard inventories
- Data quality reports

---

## Delivery Documentation

- Existing roadmaps
- Product backlogs
- Previous discovery outputs
- Lessons learned
- Open risks and issues

---

# 3.4 Define the Engagement

Every engagement should begin by answering several fundamental questions.

## Why are we here?

Understand the business driver.

Examples include:

- New strategic initiative
- Business transformation
- Technology replacement
- Data platform migration
- AI opportunity assessment
- Reporting improvements
- Regulatory requirements

A technology, reporting or AI request may be the trigger for discovery, but it should initially be treated as a proposed intervention rather than the business objective. Discovery should establish the underlying outcome, decision, information need and value before confirming a solution direction.

---

## What problem are we trying to solve?

Document the problem statement.

Examples:

- Finance reporting takes too long.
- Sales teams cannot trust reporting.
- Customer data is fragmented.
- Programme performance cannot be measured consistently.

---

## What outcomes are expected?

Define measurable outcomes.

Examples:

- Reduce reporting effort.
- Improve forecast accuracy.
- Increase data quality.
- Improve business decision making.
- Improve a defined business outcome where AI may be one possible intervention.

---

# 3.5 Define Scope

Clearly document what is included and excluded.

## In Scope

- Business functions
- Systems
- Reports
- Processes
- Teams
- Geographic regions

---

## Out of Scope

Explicitly identify what will not be covered.

This avoids misunderstanding later in the engagement.

---

# 3.6 Identify Stakeholders

Discovery depends upon engaging the right people.

Identify:

- Executive Sponsor
- Business Owner
- Product Owner
- Process Owner
- Data Owner
- System Owner
- SME
- Operational Users
- Reporting Consumers
- Technical Teams

Document:

- Responsibilities
- Availability
- Influence
- Interest

---

# 3.7 Build an Initial Understanding

Before workshops, develop an initial view of the business.

Capture:

- Business capabilities
- Core processes
- Major systems
- High-level information flows
- Known pain points
- Existing reports

This initial understanding should be treated as a hypothesis rather than fact.

Discovery exists to validate or challenge these assumptions.

---

# 3.8 Develop Discovery Hypotheses

Good discovery starts with hypotheses.

Examples include:

- Manual reporting is creating unnecessary effort.
- Multiple teams calculate KPIs differently.
- Business definitions are inconsistent.
- Duplicate data exists across systems.
- Existing dashboards are underutilised.
- AI could automate repetitive administrative tasks.

These hypotheses guide questioning but should never bias the outcome.

---

# 3.9 Plan the Engagement

Develop a lightweight engagement plan.

Include:

- Objectives
- Timeline
- Workshops
- Stakeholder interviews
- Document reviews
- Playback sessions
- Executive review
- Deliverables

---

# 3.10 Prepare Workshops

Every workshop should have:

- Purpose
- Objectives
- Agenda
- Participants
- Pre-reading
- Expected outputs
- Facilitator
- Timekeeper
- Scribe

Preparation should also include:

- Whiteboards
- Miro boards
- Templates
- Sticky notes
- Process maps
- Capability maps

---

# 3.11 Prepare Discovery Questions

Questions should be tailored for each audience.

Examples:

## Executive

- What strategic outcomes are most important?
- What concerns you most?
- Where do you believe the business could improve?

---

## Business Users

- Walk me through your day.
- What takes most of your time?
- What frustrates you?

---

## Data Teams

- Which datasets are trusted?
- Where are the quality issues?
- Which reports generate the most questions?

---

## Technology Teams

- Which systems are business critical?
- Which integrations are fragile?
- What technical debt exists?

---

# 3.12 Prepare Success Measures

Agree how discovery success will be measured.

Possible measures include:

- Critical stakeholders and perspectives are represented.
- The business outcome and scope are sufficiently clear to guide investigation.
- Important assumptions and uncertainties are visible.
- Priority capabilities, processes and decisions to investigate are identified.
- Required evidence sources and knowledge gaps are understood.
- The next discovery activities are agreed and appropriately focused.
- Stakeholders share sufficient confidence in the framing to proceed.

Completion of documents or artefacts may support these outcomes, but document production is not itself the measure of successful discovery.

---

# 3.13 Risks

Common preparation risks include:

- Undefined scope
- Missing stakeholders
- Poor executive sponsorship
- Lack of documentation
- Conflicting objectives
- Insufficient time
- Stakeholder availability
- Existing political tensions

Identify mitigation actions before workshops begin.

---

# 3.14 Deliverables

The preparation phase should produce the minimum artefacts needed to establish a well-framed engagement. Depending on the context, these may include:

- Discovery Brief
- Scope Definition
- Stakeholder Register
- Engagement Plan
- Workshop Schedule
- Initial Capability Map
- Initial System Landscape
- Initial Hypothesis / Assumption Register
- Evidence Register
- Open Question Log
- Risk Register

Do not create every artefact by default. Select those that help the team understand the problem, expose uncertainty and prepare the next discovery activity.

---

# 3.15 Exit Criteria

Preparation has reached sufficient confidence to move forward when:

- Objectives are understood sufficiently to guide investigation.
- Scope is agreed or material scope uncertainties are visible.
- Critical stakeholders are identified.
- Appropriate discovery activities are planned.
- Available documentation and evidence have been reviewed to an appropriate level.
- Discovery questions are prepared.
- Material risks and assumptions are visible.
- Sponsorship and ownership are sufficient for the next step.

DISCOVER is iterative. The team may begin Investigate once no critical preparation gap prevents useful learning, while continuing to refine scope, stakeholders, questions and evidence as new information emerges.

---

# Chapter Summary

Effective preparation creates the foundation for successful discovery.

By understanding the business context, defining scope, identifying stakeholders and preparing structured workshops, the discovery team can maximise the value of stakeholder engagement while reducing unnecessary effort and rework.
