# People

> *"Businesses do not make decisions—people do. Understanding the people behind the work is fundamental to successful discovery."*

## Purpose

The **People** perspective focuses on the individuals, roles, teams and communities that perform work, make decisions, create information, apply judgement and experience the consequences of change.

DISCOVER does not reduce People to an organisation chart or stakeholder list. It seeks to understand how work is really accomplished: who knows what, who decides what, where accountability sits, how people collaborate, which skills are scarce, where capacity is constrained and why workarounds have emerged.

Effective discovery starts by understanding people in context before assuming that a process, data or technology change will solve the problem.

## Why This Matters

People:

- create and interpret information;
- execute and adapt processes;
- make and approve decisions;
- own business outcomes;
- define and apply business rules;
- operate systems and spreadsheets;
- compensate for gaps in technology;
- hold tacit knowledge that may not be documented;
- determine whether a new data product or AI capability is trusted and adopted.

A technically elegant intervention can fail if ownership is unclear, users do not trust the information, decision rights conflict, specialist knowledge is concentrated in one person, or the proposed change makes somebody's work harder.

Understanding People helps discovery identify root causes rather than blaming behaviour that is actually a rational response to the current environment.

## Discovery Objectives

A strong People discovery should establish:

- the roles involved in the capability or outcome;
- responsibilities and accountabilities;
- decision makers, approvers and escalation paths;
- information creators, stewards, owners and consumers;
- process ownership;
- key subject-matter experts and knowledge holders;
- collaboration patterns and hand-offs;
- capacity, workload and time pressure;
- incentives and behaviours that influence outcomes;
- skills, confidence and data literacy;
- adoption needs and likely change impacts;
- key-person dependencies and resilience risks.

## What Good Looks Like

At the end of discovery you should be able to answer:

- Who performs the work?
- Who is accountable for the outcome?
- Who makes each important decision?
- Who can change a business rule?
- Who creates, owns, validates and consumes the information?
- Where do hand-offs occur between teams?
- Which people hold knowledge that is not written down?
- What happens when those people are unavailable?
- Where do capacity or time pressures affect quality?
- Which workarounds are people using and why?
- What skills or confidence are needed to use the information effectively?
- Who needs to be involved in validation and future change?

If these questions cannot be answered with sufficient confidence, People discovery is incomplete.

## What to Discover

### Roles and Responsibilities

Explore:

- business roles;
- operational roles;
- leadership roles;
- product and analytical roles;
- technology roles;
- data owner and data steward responsibilities;
- formal responsibilities versus actual practice;
- role overlap and gaps.

A useful distinction is **role** versus **person**. The role is more durable and reusable in the business model, while named individuals may be useful for immediate engagement planning.

### Decision Rights

Understand:

- who makes the decision;
- who recommends;
- who approves;
- who can override;
- who is consulted;
- who is informed;
- where decision authority is ambiguous;
- what happens when decision makers disagree.

Do not assume job seniority determines decision ownership.

### Ownership

Capture ownership of:

- capabilities;
- processes;
- business rules;
- KPIs;
- business definitions;
- data;
- reports;
- systems;
- data products;
- risks and controls.

Ownership should mean more than “the person everybody asks”. Clarify what authority and responsibility ownership actually includes.

### Collaboration and Hand-offs

Explore:

- which teams need to work together;
- communication mechanisms;
- meeting and approval dependencies;
- service relationships;
- hand-offs;
- escalation paths;
- repeated clarification;
- rework caused by misunderstanding;
- external agencies, suppliers or partners.

### Knowledge

Identify:

- recognised subject-matter experts;
- tacit knowledge;
- policy and procedural knowledge;
- local spreadsheet logic;
- undocumented exceptions;
- historical context;
- key-person dependencies;
- training material;
- knowledge that is difficult to replace.

### Capacity and Workload

Understand:

- peaks in demand;
- business-critical periods;
- recurring manual work;
- queues and backlogs;
- interruption and context switching;
- dependence on overtime;
- competing priorities;
- work that is delayed because specialist people are unavailable.

### Skills and Confidence

Consider:

- business domain knowledge;
- data literacy;
- analytical literacy;
- technical skills;
- AI literacy;
- confidence interpreting measures;
- ability to challenge data;
- training and support needs.

A sophisticated tool does not create value if the intended consumer cannot confidently use it.

### Incentives and Behaviours

Explore why people behave as they do:

- targets;
- performance measures;
- local objectives;
- risk aversion;
- incentives;
- compliance requirements;
- deadlines;
- fear of losing control;
- history of failed change;
- trust in teams or systems.

Avoid labelling behaviour as “resistance” without understanding the context.

## Generic Discovery Questions

### Understanding the Role

- What is your role and what are you accountable for?
- What does a typical day, week or month look like?
- Which activities consume the most time?
- Which activities are most important even if they take little time?
- What work arrives unexpectedly?
- Which parts of the job require the most judgement?

### Decisions

- Which decisions do you make?
- Which decisions require approval?
- Which decisions can you override?
- What information do you rely on?
- What makes the decision difficult?
- What happens when the information is late, incomplete or contradictory?
- How do you know whether the decision was good?

### Ownership

- Which processes do you own?
- Which business definitions do you own?
- Which KPIs or reports are you accountable for?
- Which data do you maintain or validate?
- Who can approve a change to the rule or definition?
- Where is ownership currently unclear?

### Collaboration

- Which teams do you depend on?
- Which teams depend on you?
- Where do hand-offs occur?
- Where do requests bounce between teams?
- Which relationships work particularly well?
- Where does misunderstanding regularly occur?
- Which external parties matter?

### Knowledge and Resilience

- Which activities depend on specialist knowledge?
- What exists only in people's heads?
- Which spreadsheets encode important judgement?
- What would happen if a key person were unavailable for a month?
- How are new team members trained?
- Which decisions are hard to delegate?

### Capacity and Friction

- Where are the busiest periods?
- Which activities are repetitive?
- What requires manual chasing?
- What creates rework?
- Where do people wait for information or approval?
- Which activity would you remove if you could?

### Change and Adoption

- What would make a proposed improvement useful to you?
- What would make you distrust it?
- What work would change?
- What new skill or behaviour would be required?
- What would need to remain under human control?
- Who would be affected indirectly?


## Evidence to Capture

Do not treat workshop conversation as automatically validated fact. Capture important findings using the DISCOVER evidence model:

- **Evidence** — explicitly supported by a trusted source, observed artefact or validated record.
- **Stakeholder Statement** — stated by a stakeholder but not yet independently validated.
- **Assumption** — plausible but unverified.
- **Interpretation** — an analytical conclusion drawn from evidence.
- **Recommendation** — a proposed intervention.
- **Open Question** — information still required.

Where evidence conflicts, record the contradiction and identify who or what can validate it. Use `UNKNOWN` when an important Golden Thread link is not established.


## Discovery Artefacts

Potential People artefacts include:

- Stakeholder Register;
- Stakeholder Map;
- RACI or responsibility view;
- Decision Catalogue;
- Ownership Register;
- Communication / collaboration map;
- Skills and Knowledge Assessment;
- Adoption / change impact assessment;
- Assumption and Open Question Log;
- Pain Point Register.

Create only those artefacts that help answer the discovery objective.

## Typical Pain Points

Common findings include:

- unclear ownership;
- duplicated responsibilities;
- key-person dependency;
- approval bottlenecks;
- knowledge silos;
- low data confidence;
- weak data literacy;
- contradictory decision rights;
- insufficient capacity;
- manual chasing;
- undocumented workarounds;
- poor cross-team communication;
- incentives that reward local rather than end-to-end outcomes;
- users excluded from solution design;
- change fatigue.

## Opportunities

Discovery may identify opportunities to:

- clarify accountability and decision rights;
- remove unnecessary approvals;
- improve delegation;
- capture critical knowledge;
- build communities of practice;
- improve data literacy;
- reduce manual work;
- redesign team interactions;
- make ownership visible;
- improve onboarding;
- introduce decision support;
- use AI for knowledge retrieval or assistance where evidence supports it;
- build products around user decisions rather than reports.

## Maturity Signals

### Fragmented

- ownership is unclear;
- knowledge sits with individuals;
- responsibilities overlap;
- manual work depends on heroics;
- adoption is reactive.

### Developing

- key roles are understood but inconsistently documented;
- some ownership exists;
- training is local;
- decision rights still vary by context.

### Defined

- roles, ownership and major decision rights are explicit;
- knowledge is shared;
- responsibilities align to processes and data;
- user needs are considered systematically.

### Managed

- capacity, adoption, skills and decision effectiveness are measured;
- ownership is actively governed;
- cross-functional collaboration is managed using evidence.

### Adaptive

- teams learn continuously;
- decision rights evolve with context;
- knowledge is resilient and reusable;
- people can confidently exploit governed data and AI capabilities.

## Finance Example

### Discovery Focus

Understand how Finance teams collaborate to deliver planning, forecasting, month-end, revenue recognition, management reporting and statutory obligations.

### Questions

**Roles and ownership**
- Which teams own forecasting, accounting, commercial finance and reporting?
- Who owns the definition of recognised revenue?
- Who can approve changes to recognition treatment?
- Which roles own reconciliations?

**Decision making**
- Who signs off forecasts?
- Who decides when an item is included or excluded?
- Who approves journals and adjustments?
- Which decisions require Finance leadership or external audit input?

**Knowledge**
- Which treatments depend on specialist accounting knowledge?
- Which recurring adjustments rely on individual memory?
- Which spreadsheets contain business logic not documented elsewhere?

**Capacity**
- Where are the month-end peaks?
- Which reconciliations consume the most effort?
- What fails when a key specialist is unavailable?

### Typical Outputs

- Finance stakeholder map;
- Finance decision catalogue;
- ownership matrix;
- key-person risk log;
- skills/adoption assessment;
- validated People findings linked to Finance Golden Threads.

## Marketing Example

### Discovery Focus

Understand how Marketing plans, approves, executes and evaluates activity across internal teams, agencies and analytical partners.

### Questions

- Who owns the campaign objective?
- Who approves budget and creative?
- Who defines the target audience?
- Who owns campaign measurement?
- Which teams interpret performance differently?
- Which knowledge sits with agencies?
- Who decides whether to optimise, stop or extend a campaign?
- Which reporting tasks are manual and who performs them?

### Typical Outputs

- Marketing stakeholder map;
- campaign decision catalogue;
- ownership and agency interaction model;
- data literacy / adoption needs;
- knowledge and dependency risks.


## Golden Thread Connection

This perspective should not exist as an isolated checklist. Connect findings to the Golden Thread wherever the evidence supports it:

**Business Strategy → Business Capability → Business Process → Business Decision → Business Information → Business Rules → Technology / Data → Data Product / Analytics → Insight / Action → Business Value**

Use stable identifiers when the engagement requires traceability:

- `STR-` Strategic Objective
- `CAP-` Business Capability
- `PRC-` Business Process
- `DEC-` Business Decision
- `INF-` Information
- `BR-` Business Rule
- `SYS-` System / Technology
- `DAT-` Data Asset
- `DP-` Data Product
- `ACT-` Business Action
- `VAL-` Business Value

Supporting discovery records may use `EVD-`, `ASM-`, `OQ-`, `PAIN-`, `OPP-` and `RSK-`.

The objective is traceability rather than bureaucracy. Do not invent links merely to complete the chain.


## Facilitation Cautions

- Do not turn People discovery into a performance assessment.
- Avoid naming individuals as “the problem”.
- Do not confuse seniority with ownership.
- Be careful with sensitive information about performance or capability.
- Ask about workarounds without judgement; they are often evidence of a system or process gap.
- Validate responsibility with more than one perspective where possible.
- Prefer role-based artefacts for durable documentation.

## Practitioner Checklist

Before completing People discovery, ask:

- [ ] Are key roles and decision makers known?
- [ ] Is accountability distinguished from participation?
- [ ] Are information and KPI owners understood?
- [ ] Have knowledge dependencies been identified?
- [ ] Have capacity and workload constraints been explored?
- [ ] Have incentives and local objectives been considered?
- [ ] Have adoption and skill needs been captured?
- [ ] Are conflicts or unknowns visible?
- [ ] Are important findings backed by evidence or clearly labelled?
- [ ] Do the People findings connect to capability, process, decision and value?

## Chapter Summary

The People perspective ensures discovery begins with the humans who create, interpret and act on business information.

By understanding roles, ownership, decision rights, collaboration, knowledge, skills and capacity, practitioners can distinguish genuine business needs from symptoms and design changes that people can trust, use and sustain.
